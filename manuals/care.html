Read the label

What the wash and dry symbols mean.

Why it matters

Every piece of clothing comes with instructions. Following them can be the difference between a shirt that lasts and one that shrinks, fades or gets ruined.1 Nobody is born knowing this. Once you can read the label, you can wash your own clothes with confidence.

Where to find it and what it says

The care label is a small tag sewn inside the clothing. Look at the inside of the collar, the neckline or the waist seam. Sometimes it is on another inside seam.1 Leave it attached, so you always know how to care for that piece.1

The picture symbols look like a secret code at first. They tell you which cycle to use, how to dry the item and what heat to use if you iron it.1 Here is how to crack the code.

The tub is washing

A picture of a wash tub means washing. Dots inside mean heat: one dot is cool, more dots are hotter. Lines underneath mean go gentle.

The square is drying

A square means drying. A circle inside the square means the dryer. Dots again show the heat. A line or a hanger means dry it flat or hang it up.

The triangle is bleach

An empty triangle means bleach is okay. A triangle with an X means no bleach at all. Either way, bleach is an adult job.

The iron is ironing

An iron with dots shows how hot it can go. An X through it means do not iron. Ask an adult before you touch an iron.

An X means no

Any symbol with an X through it means do not do that to this piece of clothing.

A circle is dry cleaning

A plain circle means the item is for a dry cleaner. Do not put it in your home washer. Hand it to an adult.

How to read a label

  1. Find the tag. Check the collar, neckline and waist seam first.1
  2. Check the tub. What temperature does it show? Is there a line under it?
  3. Check the X marks. Anything crossed out is a no.
  4. Check the square. Dryer, or hang it up? Some items shrink in the dryer, so the label matters.1
  5. Say it out loud. “Wash cool, no bleach, hang to dry.” If you can say it, you can do it.
Not sure? Ask. If a symbol looks new to you, do not guess. Show an adult, or wash the item the gentlest way and wait to learn more. Never cut the tag out, or you lose the instructions.1

Try it at home

Label hunt. Find the care label on five things you wear. Which ones say cool water? Which ones say do not put in the dryer?

Symbol match. Draw the tub, the square, the triangle and the iron on index cards. Mix them up, then say what each one means.

Make a pile. Sort five clothes into “dryer okay” and “hang to dry.”

Stop and get an adult

  • The label shows a bleach triangle, or the item is stained and you want to use bleach or a strong cleaner. Ask first. Never mix cleaning products.
  • The label says dry clean only, or you cannot read the symbols.
  • You want to use an iron or hot water. Check with an adult.
  • There is no label at all and you are not sure how to wash it.

Run a load

Sort, measure, wash, dry and fold.

Why it matters

Clean clothes are part of feeling ready for the day. Doing your own laundry is also a skill you will use every week for the rest of your life. A load takes a few steps, and once you know them, it is easy.

The steps

  1. Check pockets and close up. Empty every pocket. Shake off loose dirt and close zippers and hooks.1
  2. Sort. Whites go with whites, and lights go apart from darks. Wash towels and other fabrics that shed lint apart from the ones that attract it. Wash very dirty clothes apart from lightly dirty ones. Sorting stops colors from running onto other clothes.1
  3. Pick the temperature. Check the care label first. Cold is good for dark and bright colors that might bleed. Warm works for most clothes. Hot is for towels, bedding and very dirty items, but it can shrink or fade some fabrics.1 Check with an adult before you choose hot.
  4. Measure the detergent. Every load needs detergent. Follow the amounts on the bottle and measure before you add it. A very small load needs less, and a very big load may need more. Use detergent marked HE if the washer is a high-efficiency one.1
  5. Load it loosely. Do not pack the clothes in. Packing leaves detergent on clothes and makes extra lint.1
  6. Start the wash. Pick the cycle that fits the label and press start.
  7. Move to the dryer, or hang it. Shake damp clothes out first. Items the label says to hang go on a rack or flat. Do not overload the dryer.1
  8. Clean the lint filter. Clean the lint filter before or after each use, and never run the dryer without one.2 Ask an adult to check it with you the first time.
  9. Take clothes out when it stops. Taking them out right away means fewer wrinkles.1
  10. Fold and put away. Fold shirts and pants flat, pair the socks and put each pile where it lives. Do it while the clothes are warm and the wrinkles are easy to smooth.
The dryer needs care. The leading cause of home dryer fires is not cleaning the dryer.2 Do not leave a dryer running if you leave home or go to bed.2 Never dry anything that has touched cooking oil, gasoline, paint thinner or alcohol.2

Laundry packets are not toys

Single-load detergent packets can look like candy. Store them in their original container, out of sight and reach of young children, and do not let children handle them.3 If you have a little brother or sister at home, this one is on you. Put the container back up high every time.

Try it at home

Sorting race. With a basket of clean clothes, sort them into whites, lights and darks. How fast can you do it?

Run one load. With an adult next to you, run a small load start to finish. Measure the detergent yourself.

Fold challenge. Fold five shirts and pair five socks. Teach someone else how.

Stop and get an adult

  • Before you use bleach, a stain remover or any strong cleaning product. Ask first, and never mix products.
  • Before you pick hot water or use an iron.
  • Lint that has built up around the dryer, a dryer that takes much longer than normal, or a burning smell. Turn it off and tell an adult.2
  • Clothes with cooking oil, gasoline, paint thinner or alcohol on them. Do not put them in the dryer.2
  • A laundry packet or detergent that a young child touched or swallowed. Tell an adult right away. They can call Poison Help at 1-800-222-1222.
  • Water on the floor, strange noises or a smell from the machine. Stop it and tell an adult.

Everyday routines

Simple habits that stick: showering, deodorant, teeth, hair, nails, clean clothes and sleep.

Why it matters

As you get older, your body changes. You may sweat more and notice new smells. That is normal, and it happens to everyone. A few simple habits help you feel fresh, healthy and ready for the day. Small habits you do every day beat big efforts you do once in a while.

Build a routine

Shower or wash up

Wash your body with soap and water every day or two, and after sports or heavy sweating. Pay attention to underarms, feet and your private areas. Dry well, especially between your toes.

Deodorant

Use it on clean, dry underarms after you shower. Ask an adult to help you pick one that does not bother your skin.

Teeth

Brush twice a day with a soft brush and fluoride toothpaste.5 Aim for two minutes each time. Floss once a day.6 Replace your toothbrush every three or four months, or sooner if the bristles are frayed.5

Hands

Wash your hands with soap and water, and scrub for at least 20 seconds. Do it before you eat or cook and after the bathroom.4

Hair

Wash it with shampoo a few times a week, or when it feels oily or dirty. Comb or brush it before you go out.

Nails

Keep fingernails short and clean. Trim them after a shower when they are soft. Cut straight across and use clippers made for nails.

Clean clothes

Put on clean underwear and socks every day. Wear a shirt again only if it still looks and smells fresh. Put dirty clothes in the hamper, not on the floor.

Sleep

Kids ages 6 to 12 need 9 to 12 hours of sleep a day, and teens 13 to 17 need 8 to 10.7 Pick a bedtime and a short wind-down, like a shower, brushing and a book.

Make it stick

  1. Start with one habit. Pick the one that feels easiest, and do it for two weeks.
  2. Tie it to something you already do. Brush your teeth right after breakfast. Put on deodorant right after your shower.
  3. Make it easy. Keep your toothbrush, soap and deodorant where you can see them.
  4. Use a checklist. Tick it off each day. Seeing a row of checks feels great.
  5. Add the next habit. Build the routine one piece at a time.

Everyone starts somewhere

You do not need to be perfect. Missing a day is fine. Start again the next day. Every person you know has had to learn these habits, and the people who care about you will be glad you are working on them.

Try it at home

Make a morning card. Write your morning routine in order and tape it where you will see it.

Two-minute timer. Brush for two minutes by a timer or a favorite song.

Pack a go bag. Put deodorant, a clean shirt and socks in your sports bag.

Stop and get an adult

  • Skin that is red, sore, itchy or has a rash, or a smell that does not go away with washing. A doctor can help.
  • A tooth that hurts, or gums that bleed a lot.6
  • You get a cut while trimming your nails, or you need to use sharp tools such as razors. Ask for help.
  • Hot water that feels too hot. Turn it down or ask an adult to adjust it.
  • Trouble sleeping for a long time, or feeling very tired or worried. Tell an adult you trust.

Where do you find a clothing care label?

Inside the collar, the neckline or the waist seam, and sometimes on another inside seam.

What does an X through a symbol mean?

Do not do that to this piece of clothing.

Name the first two things you do before washing.

Empty the pockets and sort the clothes, whites with whites, lights apart from darks.

How much detergent do you use?

Measure it, and follow the amount on the bottle. Use HE detergent in a high-efficiency washer.

What do you do with the dryer lint filter?

Clean it before or after every load, and never run the dryer without it.

You want to use bleach on a stain. What do you do?

Stop and ask an adult first. Bleach and other strong cleaners are adult-check items, and you never mix products.

How long should you scrub your hands, and how long should you brush your teeth?

At least 20 seconds for hands. Two minutes, twice a day, for teeth.

Start with one habit, tie it to something you already do, make it easy and tick it off on a checklist. Missing a day is fine. Start again the next day.
