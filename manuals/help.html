Knowing when to ask

Some jobs are too big or too risky to do alone. This manual helps you sort what you can handle, what to ask an adult about, and when to call for help. Three short lessons and things to try at home. Asking is a skill, and it is a strong one.

Handle, ask or call

Sorting situations by who should handle them.

Why it matters

Every day something comes up. A spill, a stuck zipper, a strange smell, a friend who looks upset. You do not have to do everything yourself, and you do not have to panic either. You just need a quick way to sort it. In an emergency, people sometimes do not help because they are not sure what to do, or they think someone else will.1 If you know how to sort the problem, you can keep yourself and others safe.1

Asking is a skill, not a failure. Smart people ask all the time. Part of staying safe is knowing who to ask, and one skill from FEMA is simply to ask someone you trust to help you.1 Asking early often keeps a small problem small.

Three buckets

Put every problem in one of three buckets. When you are not sure, go up one bucket.

I can handle it

It is small, safe and you already know how. Wiping up spilled water. Finding a lost pencil. Tying a shoe. Looking up a word. Cleaning a tiny scrape the way you were taught.

I ask an adult

You are not sure, it needs a tool or a stove you have not been shown, or it just feels off. A job with a ladder. A smell you cannot place. A mean message. A stranger at the door. A friend who is very sad.

We call for help

Someone is badly hurt or will not wake up. There is fire or smoke. Someone is breaking in. You need the police or the fire department.1 Get to a safe place first, then call.

Sort it in four steps

  1. Stop and take a breath. Staying calm helps you think more clearly.1
  2. Ask: is anyone in danger right now? If yes, get safe and use the third bucket. Call for help.1
  3. Ask: do I know how to do this safely, and has an adult shown me? If both answers are yes, it may be yours to handle.
  4. Not sure? Ask. Say what you see: “I found something and I am not sure what to do.” Then wait for the answer before you touch it.

Try sorting these

  1. The milk spills on the floor. Handle it. Grab a towel.
  2. Your friend wants you to climb on the roof to get a ball. Ask an adult. Do not climb.
  3. You smell smoke in the kitchen. Leave and call. Get out first, then tell an adult and call 911 from a safe place.
  4. A cut is small and stops bleeding. Handle it with the first aid you know, and tell an adult after.
  5. A cut will not stop bleeding. Ask an adult right now, and call 911 if no adult is near.

Try it at home

Bucket game. Write ten everyday problems on paper slips. Sort them into three piles labeled Handle, Ask and Call. Compare piles with a family member. Where did you disagree? Why?

Say the words. Practice one sentence out loud: “I am not sure about this. Can you help me?” Say it three times until it feels easy.

Go up one bucket. Pick a situation you were unsure about. Which bucket felt right, and which bucket would be safer?

Stop and get an adult

  • You see fire, smoke, a gas smell or something sparking. Leave the place first, then get an adult and call 911.1
  • Someone is badly hurt or will not wake up. Call 911.1
  • A job needs a ladder, a sharp blade, a hot stove, a power tool or electricity, and no adult has said yes. Stop and ask.
  • You are not sure which bucket it goes in. Ask an adult. If you cannot reach one and someone may be in danger, call 911.

Your people

Naming the adults you can go to at home, school and nearby.

Why it matters

Asking is easy when you already know who to ask. A trusted helper can be a family member, a neighbor, a teacher, a security guard, or someone nearby who can help.1 If you make your list before you need it, you will not have to think hard when something goes wrong.

A busy adult is not a missing adult. Parents and caregivers work, drive, sit in meetings and have dead phone batteries. That does not mean they do not care. It just means you may need to try the next person on your list.

Build your list

  1. At home. Write the names of the adults who live with you or look after you, like a parent, a grandparent, an aunt, an older cousin or a caregiver.
  2. At school. Pick two adults you could go to, like your teacher, the office staff, the nurse or a counselor. Know where to find them.
  3. Nearby. Pick a neighbor, a relative or a family friend who lives close and who your family trusts. Ask a parent first. A good nearby helper is someone your family has already said is okay.
  4. Add the numbers. Next to each name, write a phone number you can reach them at.
  5. Keep it where you can find it. Many families post the list near the phone and keep a copy in a backpack.51

Know these by heart

FEMA says you should always know four things.1 Practice saying each one out loud.

  1. Your full name.
  2. Your home address. You need it to tell a 911 operator where to send help.25
  3. The names of your parents or caregivers.
  4. Important phone numbers. A parent’s cell phone, a neighbor and a grandparent.1 You can also write these on a card and keep it in your backpack.1

If the first adult is not available

  1. Try the next name on your list. Go down the list in order. It is okay to try more than one.
  2. Leave a clear message. “This is Sam. I need help with something. Please call me back at this number.” Say it slowly.
  3. Go to the nearest safe adult. At school, that is the office or a teacher. At home, it is the trusted neighbor your family named.
  4. Think about texting. When phone lines are busy, a text can get through when a call does not. FEMA suggests picking one contact for everyone in the family and texting them.4
  5. If someone is in danger, do not keep waiting. Move to the third bucket and call 911.1

Try it at home

Make a contact card. With a parent, write your full name, address, your parents’ names and three phone numbers on an index card. Put it in your backpack.

Say it three ways. Say your address out loud to a family member. Then say it again slower. Then say it with no help.

Walk the list. Ask a family member to quiz you. “What if Dad does not pick up? Who is next?”

Stop and get an adult

  • You cannot reach anyone on your list and someone is hurt or in danger. Call 911.1
  • A person who is not on your list tells you to come with them or keep a secret. Say no, walk away and tell an adult you trust.
  • Someone on the phone or at the door asks if you are home alone. Do not say yes. Tell an adult.
  • Your list is out of date, like a number changed. Tell a parent so you can fix it.

Emergencies

When to leave, when to call 911, and what to say.

Why it matters

An emergency is a time when something serious happens and immediate help is needed.1 Emergencies are scary. The good news is that you can learn what to do before one happens, so your body knows the plan.

Leave first

The most important thing in an emergency is to stay safe.1 You might see, hear or smell fire, smoke, gasoline, broken glass, flooding, a downed power line or weapons.1 If you are not in a safe place, leave the area, even if someone else is hurt. Once you are safe, you can help by getting help.1

Call 911

Call 911 when someone is badly hurt, when someone will not wake up, or when you need the police or the fire department.1 Do not call for a small cut or bruise you can take care of at home.1

  1. Get to a safe place. Then take a deep breath.1
  2. Call 911. Speak slowly and clearly.1
  3. Say what is happening. “My neighbor fell and is not moving.”1
  4. Say where you are. At home, give your address. Away from home, give the address or a nearby landmark.1
  5. Answer the questions. The operator may ask where you are, what the emergency is, whether the person is awake and breathing, and what number they can use to call you back.1
  6. Stay on the line. Do not hang up until the operator says it is okay.1
Called by accident? Do not hang up. Stay on the line and tell the operator what happened. If you hang up, they will need to send someone to check on you.1

If there is a fire

Get out and stay out. Your family plan should name a meeting place a safe distance from your home.3 Pick something specific, like a tree, a mailbox or the end of the driveway.

  1. When the smoke alarm beeps, go. Get out of the house quickly and meet at your safe spot, even if no adult is near.3
  2. Get low. Smoke is thinner near the floor. Use your usual way out, or your second way if the first is blocked.3
  3. Check doors with the back of your hand. If a door is hot, do not open it. Use another way out.3
  4. Stay out. Never go back inside a burning building, not for a pet or a toy.3
  5. Call 911 from outside. Once you are at the meeting place, call 911 and keep everyone close until firefighters arrive.3

Try it at home

Pretend 911. With an adult, role-play a call. One of you is the operator and asks the questions above. Do not use a real phone for this.1

Fire drill. Walk out of your home with your family, by two different ways if you can. Walk to your meeting place and name it out loud. USFA says everyone in the home should also know how to call 911 and give the address.2

Deep breaths. Breathe in for a count of three and out for a count of three. Do it five times. It is a tool you can use any time you feel scared.1

Stop and get an adult

  • You see or smell smoke or fire. Get out and stay out, then call 911 from a safe place.3
  • Someone is badly hurt or will not wake up. Call 911 and get an adult.1
  • You smell gas or see a downed power line, flooding or leaking fluid. Stay far away, leave the area and tell an adult.1
  • You feel too scared or shaky to think. Take deep breaths, get to a safe place and find an adult. Talk about your feelings afterward.1
  • You are not sure if it is an emergency. Ask an adult. If no adult is near and someone may be in danger, call 911.
