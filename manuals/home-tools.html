Meet your toolbox

What each common hand tool is for, and how to hold it.

Why it matters

A toolbox is a team. Each tool is great at one job. When you know the job, you grab the right tool, and the work gets easier and safer. Using the right tool for the right job is one of the first rules kids learn in 4-H woodworking.1 A screwdriver is not a chisel. A wrench is not a hammer.

You are old enough for most of these. 4-H lets beginners, starting at age five, learn the hammer, screwdriver, pliers, tape measure, square, sanding block, hand drill and handsaw, with an adult close by. Power tools come later.2

The six you will use most

Tape measure

For: finding out how long or wide something is.

Hold it: hook the metal end on the edge, pull the tape tight, press the lock, and read the line closest to your mark.

Hammer

For: driving nails in. The claw on the back pulls them out.

Hold it: near the end of the handle, not up by the head. Your arm stays straight and your wrist does the work.1

Phillips screwdriver

For: screws with a cross, like a plus sign, on top.

Hold it: tip straight down into the screw, one hand on the handle, the other steadying the shaft. Press in while you turn so it does not slip.

Flat screwdriver

For: screws with one straight slot on top.

Hold it: pick the tip that fills the slot. Clockwise tightens, counterclockwise loosens.1 People say “righty tighty, lefty loosey.”

Adjustable wrench

For: gripping nuts and bolts.

Hold it: spin the little wheel until the jaws hug the nut with no wiggle, then pull toward you. A loose fit rounds off the corners.

Level

For: telling you if something is straight.

Hold it: set it flat on top of the thing. When the bubble floats right between the two lines, it is level.

Two more that live in every toolbox: a pencil, for marking where things go, and pliers, which hold a nail you are about to start so your fingers stay out of the way.1 And safety glasses. Put them on before you swing anything.

Reading a tape measure

The big numbers are inches. Twelve inches make a foot. The longest line between two numbers is the half inch. The next longest are quarters. Shorter still are eighths, and the tiniest lines are sixteenths. The more lines a tool has between the inches, the more precise it is.1 So the line just past 3 in the picture reads “three and one sixteenth.” Write it 3 1⁄16″. The little mark ″ means inches and ′ means feet.1

The hook on the end wiggles a little. That is on purpose. It slides by exactly its own thickness, so you get the same answer whether you hook it over an edge or push it against a wall.

Tape measures get checked under a microscope. At the National Institute of Standards and Technology, scientists line up a microscope on the very center of each hash mark. A six-foot tape used for buying and selling has to be right to within 1⁄32 of an inch.3

Hammer a nail without hitting your finger

  1. Safety glasses on.1
  2. Hold the nail with pliers, or push it through a strip of stiff paper and hold the paper. Your fingers stay away from the head.1
  3. Hold the hammer near the end of the handle. Arm straight, wrist loose.1
  4. Tap the nail gently and square on the head until it stands by itself.1
  5. Now swing a little harder, still square, until the head sits flat on the wood. Go easy on the last hit so you do not dent the board.1
  6. Bent it? Pull it with the claw. Slip a scrap of wood under the hammer head first so it does not mark the board.1

Hard wood? Ask your adult to drill a small starter hole first.1

The toolbox rules

These are the same rules 4-H gives kids before they pick up a tool.1

  • Ask first. Tell an adult what you are doing and where.
  • Clear the table and the floor. Pick up dropped nails and screws right away.
  • Work where you can see. Good light, and fresh air if you are sanding.
  • Glasses on, sleeves up, long hair back.
  • Right tool, right job.
  • Check the tool. A wobbly hammer head, a cracked handle or a frayed cord means put it down and show an adult.
  • Put tools back when you finish, so they are there next time.

Try it at home

Toolbox tour. Find all six tools in your house. If one is missing, that is good to know too.

Measure three things. A book, a door, your own foot. Write each one down to the nearest quarter inch. Have someone check you.

Three nails. With an adult, drive three nails into a scrap board. Count your hits. Fewer hits, straighter nail, is the game.

Stop and get an adult

  • Any power tool. Drills, saws and sanders are an adult-helping job, every time.1
  • Any cord that is cracked, frayed or taped up.1
  • Anything electrical. Fire safety experts say electrical work is for a qualified electrician.6
  • A tool that is broken or loose. Show it, do not use it.

Small fixes

Tighten a loose handle, hang something level, change a light bulb.

Why it matters

Small loose things get worse. A loose knob becomes a lost knob. A crooked picture bugs everyone who walks by. Fixing the small stuff yourself takes five minutes, and it feels great. These three fixes are the ones every home needs most.

Fix 1: Tighten a loose handle or knob

  1. Find the screw. On a drawer knob it is usually inside the drawer. On a cabinet door it is on the inside of the door. On a pot handle it is at the end of the handle.
  2. Look at the screw head. Cross means Phillips. One slot means flat. Pick the tip that fills it.
  3. Hold the knob still with one hand. Turn the screw clockwise with the other until it stops.1
  4. Stop when it is snug. Snug means it stops turning easily. Cranking harder can strip the hole or crack the knob.
  5. Wiggle test. No wiggle, you are done.

If the screw spins and spins and never gets tight, the hole is stripped. That is an adult fix.

Fix 2: Hang something level

This is for light things: a poster, a small picture, a calendar. Heavy things come after.

  1. Ask where it can go. Walls have pipes and wires inside, so an adult picks the spot.
  2. Hold it up. Have someone step back and say “a little left” until it looks right. Make a small pencil mark at the top center.
  3. Measure from the top of the frame down to the hook or wire on the back. Measure that same distance down from your mark. That is where the nail goes.
  4. Tap in a small nail or picture hook, tilted slightly upward, using the hammer steps from Lesson 1.
  5. Hang it. Set the level on top. Nudge one side until the bubble sits between the lines.

Heavy things get anchored

Shelves, mirrors, TVs, dressers and bookcases are not a nail job. The Consumer Product Safety Commission says to anchor all furniture with drawers, doors and shelves to the wall, and TVs too, because tip-overs happen “in the blink of an eye.”4 In the United States a child goes to the emergency room every 24 minutes because of a furniture or TV tip-over.5

An adult does the anchoring, because the bracket has to go into a stud, the wood post inside the wall. You can be the helper. The steps are: attach a bracket to the wall, move the furniture close, attach the other bracket to the furniture, connect the strap, tighten it, then tug on the straps to make sure everything is secure.4 You hand over the tools, hold the level and do the tug test.

Two more things you can do yourself: keep remotes and toys off the top of tall furniture, so nobody climbs for them, and if a TV cannot be anchored, it belongs on a low, sturdy stand pushed all the way back.5

Fix 3: Change a light bulb

  1. Ask first, and have the adult nearby.
  2. Turn the lamp or light switch off.
  3. Wait for the bulb to cool. A hot bulb burns fingers, and a hot bulb near cloth or paper can start a fire.6
  4. Dry hands. If you need to reach, use a sturdy step stool with the adult holding it. No chairs, no stacks of boxes.
  5. Unscrew the old bulb counterclockwise. Gentle. Hold it by the glass or plastic, not the metal.
  6. Read the sticker on the lamp. It tells you the most watts the lamp can take. Your new bulb uses that number or less.6
  7. Screw the new bulb in clockwise until it is snug, not forced. Switch on.
  8. Ask the adult what to do with the old one. Wrap a burned-out bulb in paper before it goes in the trash, and some kinds need special recycling.
Pick LED when you can. LED bulbs use up to 90% less energy than old incandescent bulbs and last far longer. Incandescents give off 90% of their energy as heat. LEDs also do not burn out suddenly; they slowly dim, so you have time to buy a new one.7

Try it at home

Knob patrol. Walk through the kitchen and wiggle every knob and handle. Tighten the loose ones. Count how many you fixed.

Level check. Put the level on top of three pictures in your house. Fix the crooked ones.

Sticker hunt. Find the watt sticker on two lamps. Then read the box of a spare bulb. Would it be safe in each lamp?

Stop and get an adult

Tell an adult right away if you ever notice one of these. They are the signs fire safety experts say mean call a qualified electrician or the landlord:6

  • A breaker that keeps tripping or fuses that keep blowing.
  • A tingle when you touch something plugged in.
  • A wall outlet that feels warm or looks brown or dark.
  • A burning or rubbery smell from anything with a cord.
  • Lights that flicker or dim on their own.
  • Sparks from an outlet.

Never open an outlet, a switch or a light fixture, and never touch anything electrical with wet hands or while standing in water.

When water goes wrong

Find the shutoff valve, and know what to do first during a leak.

Why it matters

Water does not wait. A burst hose under the sink can soak a whole kitchen floor in minutes. The difference between a mess and a disaster is knowing where the shutoff is and getting there fast. The federal emergency site Ready.gov puts it simply: everyone in the household should learn how to shut off the main water valve.8 That includes you.

Leaks are also sneaky. Household leaks in the United States waste nearly one trillion gallons of water a year.10 You can learn to catch them.

Find the shutoffs (with an adult)

  1. The main valve. It is usually where the water pipe comes into the house, often in the basement, a utility room or the garage. Some homes have it in a box in the ground near the street.9
  2. Look at the handle. A round wheel handle closes by turning clockwise, usually several turns. A lever handle is open when it lines up with the pipe and closed when it sits across the pipe. Ask before you turn the main one.
  3. Mark it. Wrap bright tape around the handle so you can find it in the dark.9
  4. The small valves. Every sink has one or two under it, and every toilet has one on the wall behind it. These shut off just that one fixture. Find them all.
  5. Draw a map. One sheet of paper, one X for each valve. Stick it inside a cabinet door.

Your leak plan

Something is spraying or pouring. Do this, in this order.

  1. Turn off the nearest small valve: under the sink, or behind the toilet.
  2. Still coming, or you cannot find it? Go to the main valve and close it.
  3. Get an adult. Shout if you need to.
  4. Towels down, bucket under the drip, and move things off the floor.
  5. Water near an outlet, a cord, a lamp or anything plugged in? Do not step in it. Stay out of the room and get the adult.

Catch a hidden leak

The Environmental Protection Agency gives two easy tests.10

  • The meter test. Read your water meter. Then nobody uses water for two hours. Read it again. If the number changed, something is leaking.
  • The toilet test. Put a few drops of food coloring in the toilet tank. Wait ten minutes without flushing. If color shows up in the bowl, the toilet is leaking. Flush right after so the tank does not stain.

Try it at home

Valve hunt. Find the main valve and every small valve with an adult. Tape the main one. Draw your map.

Leak drill. Have an adult say “the kitchen sink is leaking.” Walk, do not run, to the right valve and point to it. Time yourself. Try again from another room.

Toilet test. Run the food coloring test on one toilet this week. Report what you found.

Stop and get an adult

  • Water coming through a ceiling or down a wall.
  • Water anywhere near electricity.
  • A hot water pipe or the water heater. Hot water burns.
  • You smell gas, or hear hissing. Leave right away. Call the gas company or 911 from outside. Do not flip switches, light anything or use a phone until you are out.11 And nobody ever turns gas back on themselves. Only a qualified professional does that.8
