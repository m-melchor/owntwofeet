For grown-ups

This manual is written to the student. Read it together the first time, then let them lead. Families have different amounts of money, and that is never something to be ashamed of, so the lessons use pretend amounts and never assume a child gets an allowance. They use cash, allowance, gifts and a savings jar, or a basic savings account that you open together. There is no investing advice and no product is recommended. Every lesson ends with a red box about when to stop and talk to you. Borrowing or lending money, losing money, and any online request for money or card numbers are adult-check items. Needs and wants

Sorting real purchases and deciding what comes first.

Why it matters

Money only goes so far, for every family. So everyone has to choose what to spend it on. Knowing the difference between a need and a want helps you make choices that fit your own situation.3 It also helps you see that you cannot have everything, so some things matter more than others.5

Every family is different. Families have different amounts of money, and that is okay. Nobody should feel bad about what they have. You may get an allowance, birthday money, a little cash for odd jobs, or none of these. This lesson works for all of it.

Two kinds of things

A need

Something you must have to stay healthy and safe, like food, a place to live, clothes for the weather and medicine.

A want

Something that would be nice to have, but you can live without it, like a game, a toy, a snack or the newest shoes.

Some things sit in the middle. You need shoes, but you may not need the shiny brand-name pair. Where the line falls can be different for different people, and that is a good thing to talk about.3

Sort it out

  1. Pick a few things. Choose five things people buy, like groceries, a bus pass, a video game, a hoodie and a movie ticket.
  2. Ask: can I stay healthy and safe without it? If no, it is a need. If yes, it is a want.
  3. Explain your pick out loud. Say why you put each one where you did. Sorting and explaining is the whole skill.3
  4. Put needs first. When money is limited, people cover needs before wants.5
  5. Ask about the want. Do I want it a lot, or only because I just saw an ad? Advertising tries to get people to spend more, but you decide when to spend and what to buy.2

Try it at home

Sort a pile. Write ten things on slips of paper and sort them into needs and wants. Do you and an adult agree on every one?

Ad detective. Watch or look at one ad. What does it want you to feel? Is the thing a need or a want?

The wait test. Next time you want something, wait one day. Do you still want it just as much?

Stop and get an adult

  • Anyone online or in a game asks you for money, card numbers, passwords or gift cards. Do not answer. Tell an adult.6
  • Someone asks to borrow your money, or you want to borrow money from someone. Talk to an adult first.
  • You are not sure whether something is a need. Ask an adult. Never guess when it is about medicine or safety.
  • You feel worried or ashamed about money. That is a good time to talk to an adult you trust.

Compare and choose

Unit prices, sales and when cheaper isn’t better.

Why it matters

The same thing can cost different amounts in different places, and the biggest sign is not always the best deal. Checking before you buy is a good habit.2 Smart shopping is so useful that middle school money programs give it a whole lesson.4

Find the unit price

A unit price tells you the cost of one ounce, one pound or one piece. It lets you compare a big box and a small box fairly. Look on the shelf tag. Stores usually print the price and the unit price together.78

  1. Find both prices. Look at the total price and the smaller unit price on the tag.8
  2. Use the same unit. Compare cents per ounce with cents per ounce, not ounces with pounds.8
  3. No unit price on the tag? Divide the price by the size. A 20 ounce box for $3.00 costs $0.15 per ounce.8
  4. Pick the lower unit price. That one gives you more for your money.7
  5. Check the waste. A bigger package only saves money if you will use all of it before it goes bad.7
Bigger is not always cheaper. Larger sizes cost less per ounce most of the time, but not always. Foods that are already cut up or shredded often cost more per ounce than the plain kind.8

Is it really a deal?

  1. Look at the real price. Take off the sale amount, then check the new price against other stores.2
  2. Ask if you needed it anyway. A sale on something you do not need is still money spent.
  3. Shop around. Check the same item at different stores and websites with an adult. Shopping around is a good habit.2
  4. Slow down on easy buys. One-click buys inside games and small add-ons are the moments to stop and think twice.2
  5. Know a bargain from a scam. Some “free” offers lead people to spend money without realizing it.2

Try it at home

Cereal showdown. At the store or with a store flyer, pick two sizes of the same food. Work out the price per ounce for each. Which wins?

Pretend shopping trip. Plan a pretend $20 snack run with an adult. Use unit prices to pick what you would buy.

Deal or not? Find a sale sign. Ask yourself: did I need this before the sale?

Stop and get an adult

  • A “free” offer, prize or game asks you to pay, or asks for a card number. Stop. Tell an adult.2
  • Another player or a website asks for your address, your school or other personal details. Stop and tell an adult.6
  • Anything in a game wants you to buy something with a card. Ask an adult before you tap.6
  • Someone wants you to lend them money, or to keep a purchase secret from your family.
  • You spent money by accident or lost some. Tell an adult. It happens, and a calm talk fixes more than hiding it.

Plan and save

A simple plan for spending, and setting some aside.

Why it matters

Saving means choosing to keep some money for later so you can reach a goal. Even a small amount counts. Money you spend on one thing is not there for another, and a plan helps you decide.5 Whether you get an allowance, gift money or a bit of cash now and then, the plan is the same.

Pick a goal

  1. Choose something specific. A skateboard, a book set or a gift for someone. Specific goals are easier to reach than “someday.”5
  2. Find the cost. Look it up with an adult, so you know how much you need.5
  3. Break it into small steps. If it costs $20 and you set aside $2 at a time, that is ten steps.1
  4. Track it. Color in a chart or mark each step so you can see your money grow.1

Three jars or envelopes

Take any money you get and split it into three places: spend, save and share. You can use jars, envelopes or even labeled boxes.

Spend

For things you want now, like a snack or a small toy. This one is yours to decide.

Save

For a bigger goal later. Pay yourself first: put money here before you spend any.5 A common starting point is 10 percent, or a dime for each dollar.1

Share

For giving, like a gift or a cause you care about, if that matters to your family.5

A simple plan

  1. Count what you have. Add up the money you get in a week or a month. If it is nothing right now, you can still practice with pretend money.
  2. Split it. Try 10 percent to save to start. Change it as your goals change.1
  3. Write it down. Make three columns: spend, save, share. Add each amount.
  4. Check in each week. Is your save jar closer to the goal?
A savings account. When your jar gets full, you can open a basic savings account together with a parent. Visiting the bank a few times a year to put your savings in and watch the balance grow can make saving feel real.1 Money in a federally insured account is protected if the bank closes.1 A parent must open and manage it with you.

Try it at home

Make your jars. Label three jars or envelopes: spend, save, share. If you have no money right now, use paper play money.

Goal poster. Draw a picture of your goal and color it in a little for every step you save.

Pretend plan. With an adult, make a plan for $10: how much to spend, save and share, and why.

Stop and get an adult

  • Anyone asks for money, card numbers, bank details or gift cards, in person or online. Tell an adult.6
  • You want to open an account. A parent must come with you. Never share account details with anyone else.
  • A friend asks to borrow money from your jar, or you want to borrow from theirs. Talk to an adult first.
  • Money from your jar is missing, or you lost some. Tell an adult. It is okay.
  • Spending feels out of control, like you cannot stop buying things, or you feel worried when you have no money. Talk to an adult you trust.
