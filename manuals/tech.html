Restart, update, check

A simple routine that fixes most device problems.

Why it matters

Phones, tablets and computers act up. A game freezes. The screen goes black. The Wi-Fi drops. Most of the time the fix is small, and you can do it yourself in a few minutes. Knowing the routine means you do not panic, and you do not click the first thing a pop-up tells you to.

It also keeps you safe. Updates often fix security holes, so installing them soon after they show up helps protect your device.7

The routine: check, restart, update

  1. Check the basics. Is it charged or plugged in? Is the volume up? Is Wi-Fi on? Is the cable pushed in all the way?
  2. Close it and open it again. If just one app is stuck, close that app completely and reopen it.
  3. Restart the device. Hold the power button, choose Power Off or Restart, wait about 30 seconds, then turn it back on. This clears out small glitches.
  4. Update. Open Settings and look for Software Update or System Update. Do not ignore update alerts that come from your device’s own settings.7 Ask an adult to turn on automatic updates, so new updates install as soon as they are ready.7
  5. Check the connection. Turn Wi-Fi off and on. Move closer to the router. If nothing online works on any device, ask an adult to restart the router.
  6. Still stuck? Write down what it says on the screen and what you were doing. Then show an adult. A clear note helps them fix it fast.
A pop-up is not a repair shop. A real security warning never tells you to call a phone number. If a pop-up says your computer has a virus and tells you to call, it is a scam.3 Close the browser, or hold the power button to shut the device down, and tell an adult.

What a real fix looks like

  • It happens inside your device’s own Settings, or on a company’s website that you or an adult typed in yourself.
  • Nobody rushes you.
  • Nobody asks for a password, a code or money.

If you think your computer really has a problem, the safe move is to update your security software and run a scan, or to ask a store or company your family already knows and trusts.3

Try it at home

Run the routine. With an adult, pick a device that is a little slow. Check, restart, then look for updates. Did it get better?

Find the settings. Find Software Update on a phone or computer at home. Say out loud where it is, then show someone.

Make a fix card. Write the six steps on an index card and keep it near the family computer.

Stop and get an adult

  • A pop-up or a call that says your device has a virus, and tells you to call a number.3
  • Anyone who contacted you out of the blue and asks to control your computer. Never allow it.3
  • Anyone who wants you to pay with a gift card, a payment app or cryptocurrency. Never pay.3
  • Any message asking for a password or a code, or any link in a message you did not expect. Do not click.
  • A battery that swells, gets very hot or smells odd. Stop using it and tell an adult right away.
  • You clicked something and now you feel worried. Tell an adult right away. You are not in trouble, and it happens to grown-ups too.

Locks that work

Passphrases, two-step sign-in and never sharing a password.

Why it matters

Your accounts hold your games, your messages, your photos and your friends’ names. A password is the lock on that door. A short or easy lock gets guessed. The same lock on every door means one slip opens everything.

Two things make a real difference: a strong passphrase for each account, and a second step when you sign in.

Build a passphrase

A passphrase is a few words strung together. It is longer than a normal password, so it is harder to guess, and it is easier to remember. Cybersecurity experts at CISA suggest a passphrase of four to seven unrelated words, and at least 16 characters in all. Longer is stronger.4

  1. Pick four to seven ordinary words that do not belong together. Skip names, birthdays, your pet and famous sports moments, because people can guess those.8
  2. Make them mean something only to you, like a picture in your head. A silly scene is easy to remember.8
  3. Check the length. At least 16 characters.4
  4. Use a different passphrase for every account. If one is stolen, the others stay locked.4
  5. Do not write it where anyone can see it, and do not type it into a site you got to from a message or a link.

That is a lot to remember. A password manager is an app that makes and stores strong, different passwords for you, so you only remember one.9 Setting one up is a job for you and an adult together.

Add a second step

Two-step sign-in (also called two-factor or multifactor) asks for something more than your password, like a code from an app or a text, or a fingerprint. If a thief steals your password, they still cannot get past the second step.5

  • Turn it on for every account that offers it, with an adult.5
  • Start with email. If someone gets into an email account, they can use it to reach other accounts.5
  • Look in the account’s security settings for “two-step” or “two-factor.”5

Never share a password or a code

Not with a friend, not with someone in a game, not with “support.” Real companies do not ask for your password. A code that arrives on your phone is the second lock, and handing it over opens the door for whoever asked.

Sharing with a parent or guardian you trust is different. Many families keep a safety copy of passwords for kids’ accounts, and that is a smart safety net, not a sign you did something wrong.

If a friend asks for your password “just to help with a level,” the answer is a kind no. A real friend will get it.

Try it at home

Silly sentence. Make a passphrase out of four strange words and a picture in your head. Do not use it for a real account. Just practice. Then count the letters.

Lock hunt. With an adult, find out which accounts in your house offer two-step sign-in. Turn it on for one.

Say no. Practice saying “No, I do not share my password” out loud, in a friendly way.

Stop and get an adult

  • Someone asks for a password or a code, even someone who says they are from the game, the school or the company. Never share it.
  • A message says your account is locked and sends you a link to log in. Do not click. Tell an adult.6
  • You get a sign-in code you did not ask for. Someone may be trying to get in. Tell an adult right away.
  • You shared a password by mistake. Tell an adult now so they can change it. No shame.

Spot the scam

The red flags in fake prizes, account alerts and “new number” texts.

Why it matters

Scammers want your money, your passwords or your information, and they are good at sounding friendly, official or urgent. Kids and grown-ups both get tricked. The trick is not to be perfect. The trick is to know the red flags and to pause before you click, pay or reply.

The red flags

It is rushed

“Act now.” “Last chance.” Scammers want you to act before you think. Honest businesses give you time.2 Urgent or scary wording is a warning sign.6

You did not expect it

A prize you never entered. A package you never ordered. An “account problem” from a company you do not use. If you have no account with them, treat it as suspicious.1

It asks for private info

Passwords, codes, birthdays, your address, card numbers. Messages that ask to “confirm your details” are a classic.16

It wants a strange payment

Gift cards, payment apps, cryptocurrency or wire transfers. Never pay anyone who insists on these.2

The link or sender looks off

A misspelled brand name, a strange address, a shortened link you cannot read.6 Perfect spelling does not make a message safe, because tools can write polished messages now.6

“Hi, it’s me, new number”

A text from an unknown number that acts like it knows you, then asks for a favor or money. Do not reply. Ask an adult to check with the real person using the number you already have.

What to do

  1. Pause. Do not click, reply or pay. Nothing is so urgent that it cannot wait for an adult.
  2. Do not use the links or numbers in the message. If you want to check, an adult can contact the company or person the way they already trust, like the official website or a number they know.61
  3. Show an adult. Screenshots are great. Do not delete the message until they have seen it.
  4. Report it. An adult can report scams at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, and a text can be forwarded to 7726 (SPAM).1 Internet crimes can also go to the FBI’s Internet Crime Complaint Center.10
  5. Then delete it, without clicking anything inside.6

If you already clicked

Tell an adult right away. It is not your fault, and the sooner they know, the more they can do. They may need to change a password, update security software and run a scan, or watch an account for strange activity.1 Scammers count on people feeling too embarrassed to say anything. Do not let them win that way.

Try it at home

Scam or safe? Read these out loud and name the red flags. (1) “You won a free game console! Click here in the next 10 minutes to claim.” (2) “Hi, this is Mom, I got a new number, can you buy me a gift card?” (3) A notice from your school app, in the app, about picture day.

Answers. (1) Rushed, a prize you never entered, a link. (2) New number, a favor, a gift card. (3) Looks safe: it is inside the app you already use, with no rush and no request for secrets or money.

Make a family code word. Pick a secret word with your family. Anyone who texts “it’s me” in an emergency has to know it.

Stop and get an adult

  • Anyone asks you for money, gift cards or a payment. Never pay.2
  • Anyone threatens you, scares you or says you will get in trouble.
  • Anyone tells you to keep it a secret, or says not to tell your parents.
  • Any code someone asks you to read out. Never share a code.
  • Any link in a message you did not expect. Do not click.6
  • Money or information is already gone. Tell an adult right away, with no shame.
