      <span class="num">Lesson 1</span>
      <h2 class="h2">Restart, update, check</h2>
      <p class="sub">A simple routine that fixes most device problems.</p>

      <h3 class="h3">Why it matters</h3>
      <p>Phones, tablets and computers act up. A game freezes. The screen goes black. The Wi-Fi drops. Most of the time the fix is small, and you can do it yourself in a few minutes. Knowing the routine means you do not panic, and you do not click the first thing a pop-up tells you to.</p>
      <p>It also keeps you safe. Updates often fix security holes, so installing them soon after they show up helps protect your device.<sup class="c"><a href="#s7">7</a></sup></p>

      <h3 class="h3">The routine: check, restart, update</h3>
      <ol class="steps">
        <li><b>Check the basics.</b> Is it charged or plugged in? Is the volume up? Is Wi-Fi on? Is the cable pushed in all the way?</li>
        <li><b>Close it and open it again.</b> If just one app is stuck, close that app completely and reopen it.</li>
        <li><b>Restart the device.</b> Hold the power button, choose Power Off or Restart, wait about 30 seconds, then turn it back on. This clears out small glitches.</li>
        <li><b>Update.</b> Open Settings and look for Software Update or System Update. Do not ignore update alerts that come from your device’s own settings.<sup class="c"><a href="#s7">7</a></sup> Ask an adult to turn on automatic updates, so new updates install as soon as they are ready.<sup class="c"><a href="#s7">7</a></sup></li>
        <li><b>Check the connection.</b> Turn Wi-Fi off and on. Move closer to the router. If nothing online works on any device, ask an adult to restart the router.</li>
        <li><b>Still stuck?</b> Write down what it says on the screen and what you were doing. Then show an adult. A clear note helps them fix it fast.</li>
      </ol>

      <div class="fact"><div><b>A pop-up is not a repair shop.</b> A real security warning never tells you to call a phone number. If a pop-up says your computer has a virus and tells you to call, it is a scam.<sup class="c"><a href="#s3">3</a></sup> Close the browser, or hold the power button to shut the device down, and tell an adult.</div></div>

      <h3 class="h3">What a real fix looks like</h3>
      <ul class="plain">
        <li>It happens inside your device’s own Settings, or on a company’s website that you or an adult typed in yourself.</li>
        <li>Nobody rushes you.</li>
        <li>Nobody asks for a password, a code or money.</li>
      </ul>
      <p style="margin-top:12px">If you think your computer really has a problem, the safe move is to update your security software and run a scan, or to ask a store or company your family already knows and trusts.<sup class="c"><a href="#s3">3</a></sup></p>

      <div class="panel sage try box">
        <h3>Try it at home</h3>
        <p><b>Run the routine.</b> With an adult, pick a device that is a little slow. Check, restart, then look for updates. Did it get better?</p>
        <p><b>Find the settings.</b> Find Software Update on a phone or computer at home. Say out loud where it is, then show someone.</p>
        <p><b>Make a fix card.</b> Write the six steps on an index card and keep it near the family computer.</p>
      </div>

      <div class="panel stop box white">
        <h3>Stop and get an adult</h3>
        <ul class="plain">
          <li>A pop-up or a call that says your device has a virus, and tells you to call a number.<sup class="c"><a href="#s3">3</a></sup></li>
          <li>Anyone who contacted you out of the blue and asks to control your computer. Never allow it.<sup class="c"><a href="#s3">3</a></sup></li>
          <li>Anyone who wants you to pay with a gift card, a payment app or cryptocurrency. Never pay.<sup class="c"><a href="#s3">3</a></sup></li>
          <li>Any message asking for a password or a code, or any link in a message you did not expect. Do not click.</li>
          <li>A battery that swells, gets very hot or smells odd. Stop using it and tell an adult right away.</li>
          <li>You clicked something and now you feel worried. Tell an adult right away. You are not in trouble, and it happens to grown-ups too.</li>
        </ul>
      </div>

      <span class="num">Lesson 2</span>
      <h2 class="h2">Locks that work</h2>
      <p class="sub">Passphrases, two-step sign-in and never sharing a password.</p>

      <h3 class="h3">Why it matters</h3>
      <p>Your accounts hold your games, your messages, your photos and your friends’ names. A password is the lock on that door. A short or easy lock gets guessed. The same lock on every door means one slip opens everything.</p>
      <p>Two things make a real difference: a strong passphrase for each account, and a second step when you sign in.</p>

      <h3 class="h3">Build a passphrase</h3>
      <p>A passphrase is a few words strung together. It is longer than a normal password, so it is harder to guess, and it is easier to remember. Cybersecurity experts at CISA suggest a passphrase of four to seven unrelated words, and at least 16 characters in all. Longer is stronger.<sup class="c"><a href="#s4">4</a></sup></p>
      <ol class="steps" style="margin-top:12px">
        <li>Pick four to seven ordinary words that do not belong together. Skip names, birthdays, your pet and famous sports moments, because people can guess those.<sup class="c"><a href="#s8">8</a></sup></li>
        <li>Make them mean something only to you, like a picture in your head. A silly scene is easy to remember.<sup class="c"><a href="#s8">8</a></sup></li>
        <li>Check the length. At least 16 characters.<sup class="c"><a href="#s4">4</a></sup></li>
        <li>Use a different passphrase for every account. If one is stolen, the others stay locked.<sup class="c"><a href="#s4">4</a></sup></li>
        <li>Do not write it where anyone can see it, and do not type it into a site you got to from a message or a link.</li>
      </ol>
      <p style="margin-top:12px">That is a lot to remember. A password manager is an app that makes and stores strong, different passwords for you, so you only remember one.<sup class="c"><a href="#s9">9</a></sup> Setting one up is a job for you and an adult together.</p>

      <h3 class="h3">Add a second step</h3>
      <p>Two-step sign-in (also called two-factor or multifactor) asks for something more than your password, like a code from an app or a text, or a fingerprint. If a thief steals your password, they still cannot get past the second step.<sup class="c"><a href="#s5">5</a></sup></p>
      <ul class="plain" style="margin-top:12px">
        <li>Turn it on for every account that offers it, with an adult.<sup class="c"><a href="#s5">5</a></sup></li>
        <li>Start with email. If someone gets into an email account, they can use it to reach other accounts.<sup class="c"><a href="#s5">5</a></sup></li>
        <li>Look in the account’s security settings for “two-step” or “two-factor.”<sup class="c"><a href="#s5">5</a></sup></li>
      </ul>

      <div class="panel sand box">
        <h3 class="h3">Never share a password or a code</h3>
        <p>Not with a friend, not with someone in a game, not with “support.” Real companies do not ask for your password. A code that arrives on your phone is the second lock, and handing it over opens the door for whoever asked.</p>
        <p>Sharing with a parent or guardian you trust is different. Many families keep a safety copy of passwords for kids’ accounts, and that is a smart safety net, not a sign you did something wrong.</p>
        <p>If a friend asks for your password “just to help with a level,” the answer is a kind no. A real friend will get it.</p>
      </div>

      <div class="panel sage try box">
        <h3>Try it at home</h3>
        <p><b>Silly sentence.</b> Make a passphrase out of four strange words and a picture in your head. Do not use it for a real account. Just practice. Then count the letters.</p>
        <p><b>Lock hunt.</b> With an adult, find out which accounts in your house offer two-step sign-in. Turn it on for one.</p>
        <p><b>Say no.</b> Practice saying “No, I do not share my password” out loud, in a friendly way.</p>
      </div>

      <div class="panel stop box white">
        <h3>Stop and get an adult</h3>
        <ul class="plain">
          <li>Someone asks for a password or a code, even someone who says they are from the game, the school or the company. Never share it.</li>
          <li>A message says your account is locked and sends you a link to log in. Do not click. Tell an adult.<sup class="c"><a href="#s6">6</a></sup></li>
          <li>You get a sign-in code you did not ask for. Someone may be trying to get in. Tell an adult right away.</li>
          <li>You shared a password by mistake. Tell an adult now so they can change it. No shame.</li>
        </ul>
      </div>

      <span class="num">Lesson 3</span>
      <h2 class="h2">Spot the scam</h2>
      <p class="sub">The red flags in fake prizes, account alerts and “new number” texts.</p>

      <h3 class="h3">Why it matters</h3>
      <p>Scammers want your money, your passwords or your information, and they are good at sounding friendly, official or urgent. Kids and grown-ups both get tricked. The trick is not to be perfect. The trick is to know the red flags and to pause before you click, pay or reply.</p>

      <h3 class="h3">The red flags</h3>
      <div class="tools">
        <div class="tool" style="grid-template-columns:1fr"><div><h4>It is rushed</h4><p>“Act now.” “Last chance.” Scammers want you to act before you think. Honest businesses give you time.<sup class="c"><a href="#s2">2</a></sup> Urgent or scary wording is a warning sign.<sup class="c"><a href="#s6">6</a></sup></p></div></div>
        <div class="tool" style="grid-template-columns:1fr"><div><h4>You did not expect it</h4><p>A prize you never entered. A package you never ordered. An “account problem” from a company you do not use. If you have no account with them, treat it as suspicious.<sup class="c"><a href="#s1">1</a></sup></p></div></div>
        <div class="tool" style="grid-template-columns:1fr"><div><h4>It asks for private info</h4><p>Passwords, codes, birthdays, your address, card numbers. Messages that ask to “confirm your details” are a classic.<sup class="c"><a href="#s1">1</a></sup><sup class="c"><a href="#s6">6</a></sup></p></div></div>
        <div class="tool" style="grid-template-columns:1fr"><div><h4>It wants a strange payment</h4><p>Gift cards, payment apps, cryptocurrency or wire transfers. Never pay anyone who insists on these.<sup class="c"><a href="#s2">2</a></sup></p></div></div>
        <div class="tool" style="grid-template-columns:1fr"><div><h4>The link or sender looks off</h4><p>A misspelled brand name, a strange address, a shortened link you cannot read.<sup class="c"><a href="#s6">6</a></sup> Perfect spelling does not make a message safe, because tools can write polished messages now.<sup class="c"><a href="#s6">6</a></sup></p></div></div>
        <div class="tool" style="grid-template-columns:1fr"><div><h4>“Hi, it’s me, new number”</h4><p>A text from an unknown number that acts like it knows you, then asks for a favor or money. Do not reply. Ask an adult to check with the real person using the number you already have.</p></div></div>
      </div>

      <h3 class="h3">What to do</h3>
      <ol class="steps">
        <li><b>Pause.</b> Do not click, reply or pay. Nothing is so urgent that it cannot wait for an adult.</li>
        <li><b>Do not use the links or numbers in the message.</b> If you want to check, an adult can contact the company or person the way they already trust, like the official website or a number they know.<sup class="c"><a href="#s6">6</a></sup><sup class="c"><a href="#s1">1</a></sup></li>
        <li><b>Show an adult.</b> Screenshots are great. Do not delete the message until they have seen it.</li>
        <li><b>Report it.</b> An adult can report scams at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, and a text can be forwarded to 7726 (SPAM).<sup class="c"><a href="#s1">1</a></sup> Internet crimes can also go to the FBI’s Internet Crime Complaint Center.<sup class="c"><a href="#s10">10</a></sup></li>
        <li><b>Then delete it,</b> without clicking anything inside.<sup class="c"><a href="#s6">6</a></sup></li>
      </ol>

      <div class="panel mist box">
        <h3 class="h3">If you already clicked</h3>
        <p>Tell an adult right away. It is not your fault, and the sooner they know, the more they can do. They may need to change a password, update security software and run a scan, or watch an account for strange activity.<sup class="c"><a href="#s1">1</a></sup> Scammers count on people feeling too embarrassed to say anything. Do not let them win that way.</p>
      </div>

      <div class="panel sage try box">
        <h3>Try it at home</h3>
        <p><b>Scam or safe?</b> Read these out loud and name the red flags. (1) “You won a free game console! Click here in the next 10 minutes to claim.” (2) “Hi, this is Mom, I got a new number, can you buy me a gift card?” (3) A notice from your school app, in the app, about picture day.</p>
        <p><b>Answers.</b> (1) Rushed, a prize you never entered, a link. (2) New number, a favor, a gift card. (3) Looks safe: it is inside the app you already use, with no rush and no request for secrets or money.</p>
        <p><b>Make a family code word.</b> Pick a secret word with your family. Anyone who texts “it’s me” in an emergency has to know it.</p>
      </div>

      <div class="panel stop box white">
        <h3>Stop and get an adult</h3>
        <ul class="plain">
          <li>Anyone asks you for money, gift cards or a payment. Never pay.<sup class="c"><a href="#s2">2</a></sup></li>
          <li>Anyone threatens you, scares you or says you will get in trouble.</li>
          <li>Anyone tells you to keep it a secret, or says not to tell your parents.</li>
          <li>Any code someone asks you to read out. Never share a code.</li>
          <li>Any link in a message you did not expect. Do not click.<sup class="c"><a href="#s6">6</a></sup></li>
          <li>Money or information is already gone. Tell an adult right away, with no shame.</li>
        </ul>
      </div>
